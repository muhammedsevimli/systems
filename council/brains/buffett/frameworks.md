# Buffett: frameworks

## 1. Permanent loss test
Buffett defines risk in its dictionary sense: "the possibility of loss or injury" (S6). Instead of the average of a decision on paper, he looks at whether it leaves a loss that cannot be undone if it goes wrong.
1. Write down which loss cannot be recovered if the decision turns out wrong (cash, reputation, customer trust).
2. Calculate the ratio of this loss to capital. If the ratio puts the business at risk, the decision stops.
3. Do not count recoverable losses in this test. Budget them as normal cost.
Source: S6.

## 2. Margin of safety
If you find the value only slightly above the price, Buffett does not buy (S5). In insurance the same principle enters pricing: the target price must leave a healthy margin against unforeseen social surprises (S3).
1. Calculate the expected return with a conservative assumption.
2. Write down the gap between the value from realistic assumptions and the price paid.
3. If the gap cannot absorb a clear shift in your assumptions, the margin is not enough.
Source: S5, S3.

## 3. Circle of competence
The size of the circle does not matter; knowing its edge does (S8). A complex or constantly changing business stays outside, because its future cash flow cannot be predicted (S5).
1. Write down the three assumptions the decision rests on.
2. For each one, ask: "Can I test this against past results?"
3. If an untestable assumption carries the decision, run a small trial or walk away.
Source: S8, S5.

## 4. Moat test
A strong business has a durable barrier that sustains high returns: the lowest-cost producer or a strong brand. A moat that is rebuilt all the time does not count as a moat. A business whose success depends on one super manager is not a great business either (S12).
1. Name the moat (cost, brand, network, loyalty).
2. Write down how much of the moat is left if a competitor makes the same move.
3. Decide whether the same moat will still hold in ten years.
Source: S12.

## 5. Pricing power test
Buffett's most important question: if you raise the price, does the customer leave? In the See's example, the only insight of the investment was that the business had unused pricing power (S4). The same idea is summarized in the FCIC interview (S19, summary).
1. Assume you raise the price by a small percentage.
2. Estimate the volume loss. If the loss is smaller than the extra revenue, the power exists.
3. If the power does not exist, a new product or branch finances a price war and does not produce profit.
Source: S4, S19.

## 6. Survival liquidity
Cash is oxygen for a business: unnoticed when present, the only thing on your mind when absent (S16). Berkshire keeps at least 20 billion dollars and takes on no obligation that could create a sudden cash demand (S16, S14).
1. Calculate the cash needed if revenue is zero for three months.
2. If current cash is below that, postpone the expansion decision.
3. If the new decision creates collateral, maturity or repayment demands, add them to the cash requirement.
Source: S16, S14, S15.

## 7. Retained earnings test
Every dollar reinvested must create at least one dollar of market value. Otherwise, distributing it makes more sense (S10). Against institutional momentum, extra protection is needed: projects appear in order to spend the available funds (S2, summary).
1. Write down the extra profit stream the investment will provide.
2. Compare the same money with alternative uses (paying down debt, buyback, holding it aside).
3. If the reasoning rests on the sentence "there was budget," cancel the decision.
Source: S10, S2.

## 8. Wrong boat test
Management brilliance does not fix bad economics. Which boat you board matters more than how you row (S9). Buffett finds it inappropriate to keep funding a business that is expected to lose money continually, and he closed the textile business on that reasoning.
1. Write down the return on invested capital for the last five years.
2. If the return is steadily falling and the reasoning is "it will get better later," prepare a closure plan.
3. Read the cost of closing not as a sunk cost but as the growth of the remaining loss.
Source: S9.

## 9. Mr. Market
The market is an indecisive partner who names a price every day. It serves you; it is not allowed to direct you (S1). You do not take this price as an order, you read it as an offer.
1. Write the price (a rival discount, a demand drop, panic) down as an offer.
2. Compare it with your own value calculation.
3. If your value calculation has not changed, do not change your decision. If the price gap is an opportunity, then act.
Source: S1, S13.

## 10. Reputation test
Reputation is the hardest capital item to win back. At Salomon, Buffett said he would be understanding about losing money and harsh about losing reputation (S20, summary).
1. Ask: could you explain the decision to an acquaintance who will read about it in the newspaper tomorrow? (S20, summary)
2. If there is a shortcut that reduces customer trust, take its cost as seriously as a capital loss.
Source: S20.
