# Buffett: stories

## 1. Closing the textile business (1985)
Buffett closed Berkshire's textile business in 1985, 21 years after taking control of it. In the 1978 report he had written down his reasons for continuing textiles, and on the fourth reason, "reasonable cash returns," he admitted he had been "very wrong." After 1979 the business consumed large amounts of cash. Principle: management quality does not fix the economics of a bad business, and funding a business that is expected to keep losing money makes no sense (S9).

## 2. Dexter Shoe (1993)
In 1993, Buffett bought Dexter Shoe with Berkshire stock valued at 433 million dollars. What he took for a durable competitive advantage disappeared within a few years. Paying with stock made the mistake bigger: according to the 2007 letter, the cost rose from 400 million to 3.5 billion dollars. Principle: verify the moat, and do not pay with a currency as valuable as your own stock (S6, S12).

## 3. See's Candies (pricing power)
When buying See's, Buffett and Munger relied on the insight that "the business has unused pricing power." Over twenty years the company needed only 18 million dollars of reinvestment and distributed 410 million dollars of pretax profit. Principle: the ability to raise prices produces high returns with little capital (S4).

## 4. September 2008 (cash buffer)
In September 2008, many companies were wondering whether their checks would bounce. Thanks to at least 20 billion dollars in its vault, Berkshire supplied 15.6 billion dollars of fresh money to American companies in three weeks. In his 2008 letter, Buffett had written that he would not give up even a night's sleep for the chance of extra profit. Principle: liquidity gives you the power to go on offense in a crisis (S16, S13).

## 5. Gen Re Securities (unwinding derivatives)
Berkshire began unwinding the derivatives business inside Gen Re, and even about a year later it was still carrying 6.5 billion dollars of receivables. Buffett called derivatives "time bombs" and explained that in a crisis, receivables that look independent of each other can go bad together. Principle: a complex liability is easy to enter and expensive to exit (S11).

## 6. Salomon (1991)
On August 18, 1991, Buffett was elected interim chairman of Salomon. In his testimony before Congress, he said he would be understanding if employees lost money for the firm, and harsh at the slightest loss of reputation. Principle: reputation is the most fragile item of capital (S4, S20; testimony is a summary).

## 7. The big earthquake policy (1994)
Berkshire immediately wrote a 400 million dollar policy for an insurer against a California earthquake. Buffett wrote that no one else he knew would take this risk on their own account, and said that by accepting the volatility they expected a higher long-term return. The "worst case" analysis accounts for the chance that all loss items fall in the same year. Principle: when accepting a risk, you must be able to carry the worst case (S7).

## 8. Grandfather Ernest and 1,000 dollars
In 1970, when Buffett opened his aunt Alice's safe, he found 1,000 dollars in cash next to a letter from his grandfather Ernest. Ernest knew that liquidity is necessary for survival. Berkshire turned this idea into a commitment to hold at least 10 billion dollars in cash. Principle: cash serves the same function in a family business and in a large company (S14).
