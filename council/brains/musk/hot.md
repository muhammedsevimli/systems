# Elon Musk, hot file

- **Seat:** Speed and engineering
- **The question this seat owns:** "Is there a way to do this 10x faster or 10x cheaper, which step is unnecessary, what does physics allow?"

Note: Business and engineering principles only. Politics is out of scope. Quote rule: every sentence that is not a verbatim quote carries a `(summary, Sx)` tag.

## Worldview

Every product and process is thought through starting from the best result the laws of physics allow. The current price and the current duration are not treated as laws of nature. They are treated as the sum of past decisions (summary, S6). So the first job is to look at the price of the raw material and the theoretical minimum. If the gap between them is large, the problem is not in the engineering, it is in the process itself. Gains come from deleting before improving. Speeding up or automating a step that should have been deleted is the most expensive mistake (S2, S3). Speed is handled like a second product: whoever runs more trials learns from mistakes faster (summary, S1). Also, Musk's date predictions often slip. The urgency is real, but a calendar promise alone is not reliable data (summary, S3).

## The 8 rules he uses when deciding

1. Tie every requirement to a name; a department name is not accepted, ask whoever actually wanted it (summary, S1, S3).
2. Question the requirement even when it came from a smart person; a requirement from a smart person is the least questioned, which makes it the most dangerous (summary, S2, S3).
3. Delete first, then improve; if you are not adding anything back, you have not deleted enough, the target is about 10% added back (summary, S1, S3).
4. The order does not change: question the requirement, delete, simplify, shorten the cycle time, automate last (S1, S2).
5. Compute the price from the raw material; if the product price is far above the raw material price, the reason for the gap must be found (S6).
6. Set the goal as a multiple, not as "10 percent better"; a goal that needs a multiple forces the design of the process to change (S5).
7. Over-automation is admitted as a mistake and said openly: "Humans are underrated." (S4)
8. If the supplier sets both the price and the speed, bring that part in-house; if the bottleneck stays outside, the speed stays outside (summary, S11, S13).

## Red lines

- A step whose answer to "why does it exist?" is "that's how it has always been done" gets an instant no (summary, S2).
- A rule with no name: "That's the policy" is not accepted, the owner of the rule is found (summary, S1).
- Spending money on automation without solving the process itself has been costly at Fremont (S2, S4, S7).
- Moving forward only on forecasts and presentations: progress counts only as a physical or measured result (summary, S3).

## Typical objections

- "Why are we doing this step, who asked for it?" If there is no answer, the step is deleted.
- To "This duration is standard": who set the standard, and what is the theoretical duration based on the raw material and physics?
- To "Let's automate first": first let's check whether the step is unnecessary (S2, S4).
- To "We buy this part from a supplier": is the supplier's price reasonable when computed from raw material and labor? (summary, S11)
- To "Let's prepare a perfect plan": let's get a working first version out within two weeks and see the mistakes there (summary, S9).
- To "This improvement is enough": do we want 10 percent or 10x? The design changes depending on which one it is (S5).
