# Elon Musk, frameworks

Every framework is adapted to a small business decision. Source codes are in parentheses.

## 1. "The Algorithm": the order of five steps

**What it means:** The five steps Musk applies to his teams: question the requirement, delete, simplify and optimize, shorten the cycle time, automate. The order itself is the strength of the framework. Most businesses start from the fifth step and beautifully automate a process that should not exist (S1, S2, S3).

**How to apply it:**
1. Write down the decision subject (opening a branch, order flow, a product) step by step.
2. Next to each step write "who asked for it, why"; mark the step with no answer by crossing it out.
3. Delete the marked ones, simplify the rest, measure the time and shorten it.
4. At the very end, hand the remaining step to a tool or automation if it repeats.

## 2. Requirement tied to a name

**What it means:** Every rule and every requirement is tied to a person. A "department" or a "policy" is not accepted as an owner. The question becomes something that can be asked to that person (summary, S1, S3).

**How to apply it:**
1. Look at these in your business: return conditions, minimum order amount, number of product varieties, opening hours.
2. Next to each one write who set it, when, and for what reason.
3. Suspend the one with no findable reason for a month and measure customer reaction.

## 3. The 10% add-back test

**What it means:** If you add back about 10% of what you deleted, you are standing in the right place. If you added back none, you deleted too cautiously (summary, S1, S3).

**How to apply it:**
1. For the menu, product list, or order form, make a "can be deleted" list.
2. Close all of them for 30 days; note which ones are asked for back.
3. If the share asked back is far below 10%, grow the delete list.

## 4. Raw material price test

**What it means:** Compute the product's sale price not from market perception but from the price of the materials inside it. By asking "what is the market value of the materials" about the battery, Musk showed that the current price is not mandatory (S6). The term "idiot index" (the ratio of product price to raw material price) is the same idea, the exact source of the term was not verified (summary, S3).

**How to apply it:**
1. In the product, write raw material, labor, packaging, and shipping costs separately.
2. Divide the sale price by this total.
3. If the ratio is high, question the gap by its cause: brand, process loss, or middleman margin?

## 5. Multiple goal (the 10x test)

**What it means:** A goal of "10 percent better" keeps the current design. A goal of "10x" forces the design to change. In the Boring Company example, Musk set the goal as a tenfold improvement in tunneling cost and broke it into parts such as diameter, continuous operation, and power (S5).

**How to apply it:**
1. Ask what must remain to cut a duration (for example order preparation at 40 min) by 10x.
2. Separate the components of the duration: waiting, transport, searching for information, work.
3. For each component, write the minimum that physics or the work allows, and focus on the largest gap.

## 6. The minimum physics allows

**What it means:** Compute the theoretical lower bound for time or cost; how far above this bound is the current state? If the gap comes from the process and not from physics, the gain is large (summary, S1, S6).

**How to apply it:**
1. Compare an order's working time (time actually spent processing) with its total time.
2. If working time is a small share of the total, target the waiting and handoff steps.
3. Measure the new goal weekly and post it on the wall.

## 7. Automation last

**What it means:** At Tesla Fremont, automating every step from the start slowed Model 3 production. Musk admitted this openly: "Yes, excessive automation at Tesla was a mistake. To be precise, my mistake. Humans are underrated." (S4, S7).

**How to apply it:**
1. Run the process by hand and with simple tools for one full week.
2. Among the remaining steps, pick the one that repeats every day.
3. Automate only that one; leave the one with many edge cases to people.

## 8. The do-it-yourself threshold at a supply bottleneck

**What it means:** If a part is both expensive and slow from outside, consider bringing that part in-house. SpaceX applied this systematically and built most of its products itself (summary, S11, S13).

**How to apply it:**
1. List the three inputs that are most expensive and arrive latest.
2. For each one, make an internal cost estimate from raw material and labor.
3. If the internal cost is clearly below the outside price, try it with a small pilot production.

## 9. The factory is the product

**What it means:** Designing the setup that makes the product counts as work just as much as designing the product. At Tesla, the GA4 line built inside a tent was set up in much less time and with much fewer resources than a traditional line (S9). "Urgency" in practice is this speed setting.

**How to apply it:**
1. Draw the path of an order reaching the customer (stock, packaging, shipping, returns) as a "line".
2. Find the slowest station and set up a trial layout for it within two weeks.
3. Instead of waiting for the perfect design, build the first working line, then improve it.
