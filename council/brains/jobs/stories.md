# Steve Jobs: stories

## 1. WWDC 1997: OpenDoc and "no"
In the year Jobs returned to Apple as an advisor, he answered developers' questions. The question was about OpenDoc, which he had canceled. First he acknowledged the cost by saying "I know some of you spent a lot of time working on stuff that we put a bullet in the head of" and "I feel your pain." Then he said Apple had turned into a farm full of animals heading in different directions, and closed the answer with "Focusing is about saying no." Principle: the person who states the cost of a no can defend the no. (S2)

## 2. The four squares on the board
According to Isaacson, in a product strategy meeting Jobs shouted "Stop! This is crazy." He drew four squares on the board: Consumer and Pro across the top, Desktop and Portable down the side. The task was to make one great product for each square. Schiller says the room fell into "dumb silence." Principle: a product line is a grid that splits the customer on two axes. (S4, pp. 337-338, secondhand account)

## 3. Macworld New York 1998: from 15 platforms to 4 products
Jobs described how Apple had fifteen product platforms and countless variants of each. He said four big products would be enough, so they could put "A team on every single one of them." He added that they could cut the product refresh cycle from eighteen months to nine. Principle: fewer products gain both quality and speed. (S14)

## 4. The iMac and the 2,000 dollar gap
In the same talk he said Apple had no impressive product under 2,000 dollars. The answer was the iMac, announced on May 6, 1998 at 1,299 dollars. Apple had posted a loss of 1.05 billion dollars in fiscal 1997; in 1998 it reported a profit of 309 million dollars. Principle: an empty cell in the grid is considered together with its price band. (S14, S17, S19)

## 5. The calligraphy class
In college Jobs wandered into a calligraphy class and learned about letter spacing, serif and sans serif. Years later he told how this became the typography of the first Mac: if he hadn't taken that class, the Mac would not have had multiple typefaces. Principle: experience is born where technology and humanity meet. (S1)

## 6. The first Apple Store: "Genius" and the ambush
When introducing the first store in 2001, Jobs described an idea where "if you had any questions, you could ask a Genius." In 2003 at Stanford GSB he said the goal was not to sell only to the 5 percent who already own the products, but to "ambush" new people. Principle: a store decision is made for the person you don't know yet, not for the existing customer. (S15)

## 7. iPhone: erase the buttons
At Macworld 2007 Jobs said smartphones were "not so smart and not so easy to use." He explained that the buttons and controls never change, yet every application wants an interface that suits it. He passed over the stylus question with "Who wants a stylus?" and pointed to the finger. Principle: first define the moment of use, then erase or rebuild the tool to fit it. (S8)

## 8. Gates and taste
In the 1995 Lost Interview, Jobs said his only complaint about Microsoft was lack of taste ("they just have no taste"). In 2007 on the D5 stage Gates said "I'd give a lot to have Steve's taste." Principle: taste looks abstract but is measured in the consistency of decisions. (S5, S6)
