# Jeff Bezos: stories

## 1. Calling the 1-800 number in the meeting
In a weekly business review there was a metric showing that customer line wait time was under 60 seconds, but customers said they waited long. While the head of customer service defended the metric, Bezos picked up the phone, dialed the number, and everyone waited in silence. The wait was longer than ten minutes. **Principle:** If data and the story conflict, doubt the metric first, because usually the wrong thing is being measured. (S8)

## 2. Prime: nobody asked for it, but they wanted it
In the 2016 letter Bezos writes "No customer ever asked Amazon to create the Prime membership program, but it sure turns out they wanted it" (S4). The 2013 letter recalls that nine years earlier Prime was a "new, unproven, even foolhardy by some accounts" idea: unlimited two-day shipping for a fixed annual fee (S2). The 2018 letter says that Prime and FBA were invested in "after significant financial risk and long internal debate" (S6). **Principle:** The customer cannot know to ask for a solution that does not exist yet, but after the invention the proof comes from the customer.

## 3. AWS: "no one asked for it"
In the 2018 letter, saying "No one asked for AWS. No one." about AWS, Bezos describes starting the work with a trio of intuition, curiosity and financial risk. The world was ready for this service but he did not know it (S6). **Principle:** Listening to the customer is necessary but not enough; the biggest leap comes from what the customer cannot ask for.

## 4. "Disagree and commit" at Amazon Studios
In the 2016 letter, for an Amazon Studios production that got the green light, Bezos wrote the team his doubts: how interesting it is is debatable, the production is complex, the commercial terms are weak, there are other opportunities. The team thought differently and wanted to go ahead. Bezos replied right away "I disagree and commit and hope it becomes the most watched thing we've ever made" (S4). **Principle:** State a real objection openly, then give the commitment in full; waiting to be persuaded slows the decision loop.

## 5. Speaking last in the meeting
Bezos says he speaks last in every meeting he attends. He has observed that if he speaks first, even strong, smart participants can change their views. When possible he wants the most junior person to speak first (S8). **Principle:** A mechanism is built from small arrangements that prevent seniority from suppressing the truth.

## 6. The six-page narrative and the study hall
At Amazon there are no slide presentations. The meeting starts with the silent reading, for thirty minutes at the start, of a memo in the form of a six-page narrative. According to Bezos, telling everyone to read ahead does not work, because people do not find the time and act as if they had read it (S5, S8). **Principle:** A mechanism that speeds up the quality of thought is more reliable than a promise of culture.

## 7. Paper cuts
Teams focused on big customer problems do not find time to get down to small frictions. As Bezos tells it, Amazon keeps a long list of these small problems and sets up dedicated teams that fix only those (S8). **Principle:** Customer experience is not won with big moves; you need a mechanism of regular small-defect cleanup.

## 8. The CEO who slows down before the door
When he was Amazon CEO, Bezos described himself as the "chief slow down officer": when a one-way door decision came up, he would slow it by saying "there are three more ways I can analyze this." The same person wants two-way decisions made fast by small teams (S8). **Principle:** Speed and care are separate processes for separate decisions; determining the door type joins the two.
