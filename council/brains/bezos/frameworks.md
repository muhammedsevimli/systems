# Jeff Bezos: frameworks

## 1. Working backwards (Working Backwards, PR/FAQ)
Before building the product, you write the press release that will go to the customer. At Amazon the release does not exceed one page and the FAQ section does not exceed five pages (S10). If the release cannot be written, the product has not been thought through yet.
**How to apply:** (1) Turn the decision into a customer sentence: "The customer can now do X, the Y problem is gone." (2) Write the one-page release. (3) Put the hardest questions in the FAQ: cost, who buys, why now. (4) If the release or FAQ is not convincing, the decision waits. Example: for a new branch, if you cannot write the sentence "why would a neighbor come here in the first week", it is too early to look for a location.
Source: S9, S10, S11.

## 2. One-way / two-way door
In Bezos's 2015 letter, decisions fall into two types. An irreversible decision (Type 1) is made slowly and with consultation. A reversible decision (Type 2) is made fast by one person or a small team (S3). Growing organizations run every decision like Type 1 and slow down.
**How to apply:** (1) Write the cost of reversing the decision (money, time, reputation). (2) If the cost is low, cut the time to one day and assign a single owner. (3) If it is high, ask for one more alternative analysis. When moving to online sales, opening a site is a two-way door, signing a lease is one-way.
Source: S3, S8.

## 3. The 70% rule
"Most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had." (S4) Being wrong is cheaper than being slow if you can notice the mistake fast and fix it.
**How to apply:** (1) List the information you want. (2) Separate what can be had in half a day from what takes weeks. (3) Collect the fast ones, decide without waiting for the rest, and set a date to come back.
Source: S4.

## 4. Proxy audit and the anecdote test
Over time the metric replaces the truth. According to Bezos, when data and the customer's story conflict, the story is usually right, because the wrong thing is being measured (S8).
**How to apply:** (1) For every metric you look at for the decision, write "who invented this, for which customer truth." (2) Talk to five customers or try it yourself (buy, call, complain). (3) If there is a conflict, fix the metric definition. In a pricing decision, instead of "conversion rate", look at the reasons of those who saw the price and walked away.
Source: S4, S8.

## 5. The mechanism test
Intention works once, a mechanism works every time. A quote attributed to Bezos: "Good intentions don't work, mechanisms do" (summary, S15). A mechanism is a setup with an input, an owner, a rhythm and an inspection. At Amazon these were six-page narratives, the weekly business review and paper cut lists (S5, S8).
**How to apply:** (1) Find the sentence "we will be more careful." (2) Replace it with who will do it, how often, with what output. (3) Treat a decision whose mechanism cannot be written as a one-off effort, and do not plan as if it will scale.
Source: S5, S8, S15.

## 6. The time-stable customer need test
According to Bezos, the way to find big businesses is to look at the customer need that will not change in ten years. In retail these were low prices, fast delivery and wide selection (S8).
**How to apply:** (1) Write the three things your product's customer will still want five years from now. (2) Weight the investment toward them. (3) Turn trend-driven, temporary wants into small experiments.
Source: S8.

## 7. Disagree and commit
When there is no full proof and opinions have split, state the objection openly, then move to full support. In the 2016 letter, about a series, he wrote "I disagree and commit and hope it becomes the most watched thing we've ever made" (S4). A war of fatigue and compromise do not lead to truth; when stuck, escalate the decision to a higher level (S8).
**How to apply:** (1) Write your objection in one paragraph. (2) The decision owner listens and decides. (3) The objector gives a written promise to support the goal.
Source: S4, S8, S9.

## 8. Measure, close, grow (the long-term investment rule)
The 1997 letter: measure investment programs analytically, close the ones with insufficient return, increase the ones that work (S1). A bold but measured bet. Prime started as a "new, unproven, even foolhardy by some accounts" idea (S2).
**How to apply:** (1) Write the success criterion and the closing date of the investment in advance. (2) Start with a small experiment (Amazon ran 1,976 Weblab experiments in 2013, S2). (3) Multiply what passes the criterion, close what fails with the reasons.
Source: S1, S2, S6.

## 9. Regret minimization
You make the choice by asking your eighty-year-old self: which would I regret not having done? Bezos describes applying this when he left his job at D.E. Shaw and founded Amazon (summary, S13).
**How to apply:** (1) Use it for big, life-changing decisions; do not use it for weekly operating decisions. (2) Write side by side the regret of trying and failing and the regret of never trying. (3) If never trying weighs more, take the smallest step that makes the door two-way.
Source: S13.

## 10. Balancing wandering and efficiency
"Wandering is an essential counter-balance to efficiency." (S6) But wandering is not random; it is steered by intuition and curiosity about the customer. Be efficient on the path you already know, give time to exploration in new territory.
**How to apply:** (1) Split the work into "known path" and "exploration." (2) Give exploration a fixed share (for example, one day a week). (3) Steer exploration by customer observation, not by hunch.
Source: S6, S8.
